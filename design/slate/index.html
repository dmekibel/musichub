Ten ways to see one piece

Bach's Prelude in C, seen through ten lenses from music theory. Every drawing is computed from the real score and the checked analysis. Ranked by how much each one reveals.

01

The three families

after Barry Harris · Lendvai · Metaharmony

All 12 notes split into exactly three diminished squares. Every bar is a point inside the triangle, placed by how much of each square it uses. Ordinary chords mix families and stay inside; Bach's diminished chords jump to a corner (bars 12, 14, 22) or right next to one (23 and 28, where a note from the bar before still rings), because each is a single pure square. You see the hidden skeleton Barry Harris taught.

For: Jazz players, theory lovers, anyone curious about Metaharmony's idea, now on a real piece.

Live version: Make it live: the dot moves as the music plays; tap a corner to hear the square and its four dominant children.

02

The keyscape

after Craig Sapp (Stanford)
C majord minorb minora minorD majorG majorg minorf minore minorF majorc minorE♭ major

Each tiny cell answers: if you listened only to these bars, what key would you hear? Bottom row = bar by bar; top point = the whole piece. Tall columns of one color are solid keys; the G region shows as a mountain of its own inside C. The whole tonal plan in one picture.

For: Students, analysts, people comparing pieces. Works as the piece's sleeve.

Live version: Tap a cell to play just those bars. Every piece in the archive gets one, so you can compare at a glance.

03

The reduction

after Heinrich Schenker · Lerdahl & Jackendoff

Zoom out and 35 chords become five regions, then just three: home, five, home (I–V–I), with the top line falling E, D, C (Schenker's reading). The deepest pattern in tonal music: everything else decorates one cadence.

For: Deep theory students; the 'secret pattern' moment for everyone once it animates.

Live version: This is the grain slider: drag and the chords merge into regions, then into the skeleton, with the music still playing.

04

Voice threads

after Voice leading · Tymoczko

Bach's five notes per bar as five threads. Colored dots are notes that move; grey dots hold. Each voice moves under two half steps per bar on average (9.1 across all five), and about a third of the notes simply stay put. That smoothness is why the piece sounds like one breath.

For: Pianists, arrangers, composers.

Live version: Hover a bar to see which voice carries the change; compare with a pop song's jumpy voicings.

05

The Tonnetz path

after Euler · Riemann · neo-Riemannian theory

Notes on a grid where neighbors are a fifth or a third apart. Every major or minor chord is a triangle, and chords that share two notes share an edge. The piece's path shows which moves are smooth (one step to a neighbor) and where it leaps. Diminished chords leave the grid, so the line breaks there.

For: Theory nerds, film composers (it's how many think about chord colors).

Live version: The path draws itself as it plays; triangles glow while they sound.

06

The fifths orbit

after Circle of fifths · Fourier phase (Amiot, Yust)
1 · Home2 · A trip to G3 · The way back4 · Darker colors5 · The long wait6 · Home, held

The circle of fifths, with a dot for the center of gravity of each bar's notes. Near the edge means clearly in one key; toward the center means ambiguous. The trip to G moves the whole cloud one step clockwise; the squares pull toward the middle.

For: Everyone: it shows 'where the key is' without any notation.

Live version: Ring labels light up as notes play; a transpose dial rotates the picture without changing its shape.

07

Family barcode

after Lendvai's axes as a stripe

Each bar split by how much of each square family it uses. Most bars are a mix; the five diminished bars are a single color. It's a one-line strip you can put under any other view.

For: A companion strip for any lens; quick comparison between pieces.

Live version: Sort a playlist by family balance; find songs that live in one square.

08

Chroma grid

after Pitch-class profile with bass

Every note name (rows, in fifths order) and every bar (columns), brightness = how long it sounds; the outlined cell is the bass. You can see the long wait: the G row holds for eight bars while everything above it changes.

For: Analysts and producers; the 'raw data' view.

Live version: Tap a row to hear that note's part alone.

09

The Five Dials

after Fourier components (Amiot, Yust, wavescapes)

Five measures for every bar, each one a symmetric shape on the 12-note clock. The Squares dial hits exactly 1.00 on the diminished bars (dots). These are the numbers that let the archive compare any music, from Bach to Coltrane.

For: Researchers, the archive, power users.

Live version: The same streams computed for a whole playlist become its fingerprint.

10

The Journey

after the current mockup

How far from home each bar feels, smoothed into a landscape. Clean and friendly, but it's an estimate and only one dimension.

For: Beginners: the front door.

Live version: Keep as the simplest level; every other view above is a deeper lens on the same timeline.

Metaharmony (Tom Glazebrook) inspired the three-families view; its math (Barry Harris's three diminished chords, Lendvai's axes) is public. These drawings use our own colors and forms, not his.
